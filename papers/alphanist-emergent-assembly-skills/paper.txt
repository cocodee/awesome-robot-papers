Abstract— The NIST Assembly Task Board #1 remains a
challenging benchmark for learning-based methods in robotic
manipulation. Over short horizons, it demands precise, contact-
rich control of small, tight-fitting parts. Over long horizons,
it requires recovery and coordination across extended action
sequences. Demonstrations, task decomposition, and dense
progress rewards have enabled research advances by providing
guidance on how to assemble. But how much of the strategy
must be specified? The progression from AlphaGo to AlphaGo
Zero showed that RL at scale can discover superhuman strate-
gies without prescribing how to win. AlphaNIST asks whether
a sparse binary task reward and task diversity admitted by
this task-agnostic reward can similarly let a robot discover
how to assemble. In simulation, we train a single policy from
scratch across all 20 assembly tasks on the NIST Assembly
Task Board #1, reaching 98.2% overall training success without
demonstrations, progress shaping, scripted primitives, or per-
task tuning. The framework also trains policies to complete
ten assembly pairs in a single episode without a prescribed
order, with task success rewarded only when all ten pairs
are simultaneously complete. The learned policies discover
dexterous and planning-like behaviors: choosing grasps for later
assembly, using surrounding geometry to reorient parts, and
preparing one part, assembling another, then returning to finish
the first without an explicit planner. For videos, see our website:
https://alpha-nist.github.io/
I. INTRODUCTION
Robotic assembly is a longstanding challenge, and the
NIST Assembly Task Boards are a persistent benchmark [1];
to the best of our knowledge, no learning-based approaches
have solved all tasks on any of the boards. Assembly Task
Board #1 (ATB1) consists of 20 assembly pair tasks (a
part and its fixed mating geometry on the board) that are
relevant for manufacturing, including peg insertion, gear
meshing, electrical connector insertion, and nut threading.
Solving this task board is exceptionally challenging for three
key reasons: (1) The sequence for solving the tasks might
be unspecified, and not all sequences may be efficient or
valid (e.g., solving task N may obstruct the robot from
accessing the parts in task N + 1). (2) Each task is long-
horizon, encompassing grasping, transport, and contact-rich
assembly [2]. Parts may begin in a wide range of poses
and require multiple reorientations before grasping. During
transport, parts may need further reorientation in-hand or
through extrinsic contact. During assembly, parts may slip,
become misaligned, or jam during insertion or threading [2],
requiring robust recovery and reexecution. (3) Solving the
tasks requires precise control during complex contact inter-
actions. The parts themselves are small (as small as 4 mm in
diameter), have high geometric detail (e.g., helical threads),
and have tight clearances between mating surfaces (as low
as zero). Tasks such as nut threading involve learning helical
motion during frictional contact between multiple mating
surfaces, during which a nut can tilt, cross-thread, or jam
instead of advancing along the bolt [2].
Learning-free approaches typically rely on planning and
strong human priors: assembly sequence planning [3]; cus-
tomized fixtures, adapters, and tooling [1], [4]; engineered
motion and force primitives [5]; and tuned admittance,
impedance, or force-motion controllers [4], [5].
Learning-based approaches, on the other hand, can relax
some of these constraints, but have shown progress only
within a limited scope. Most efforts have assumed a fixed
assembly sequence, heavily constrained the initial poses
of parts (e.g., beginning in an already-grasped state) [6],
assumed access to accurate pose estimates, learned specialist
rather than multi-task policies [2], addressed only a subset
of tasks on the board [6], and/or achieved only low-to-

moderate success rates by industrial standards. Success-

We draw inspiration from the landmark AlphaGo series in
reinforcement learning, which surpassed the strongest human
Go players. The progression from AlphaGo to AlphaGo Zero
showed that learning from a terminal task objective could
encourage superhuman decision-making over long horizons
without human demonstrations [8], [9]. In this paper, we
ask whether a similar approach is possible for robotics:
can sparse binary task rewards, combined with appropriate
training strategies, enable a robot to solve sequences of long-
horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks, perhaps leveraging
dexterous behaviors that no human could feasibly specify
or demonstrate? To make learning from sparse task rewards
more tractable, we take a seemingly counterintuitive step:
train jointly across heterogeneous assembly pairs differing
in geometries, tolerances, and assembly requirements (a nut
and its bolt, a gear and its gearshaft, a peg and its hole) from
a broad coverage of initial states, converting MDP diversity
into a resource rather than a burden.
Guided by this insight, we develop AlphaNIST, a learning
framework that trains a single policy under sparse binary
task rewards across all 20 tasks on the NIST Assembly
Task Board 1 (Fig. 2). The policy handles an exceptional
range of initial part poses, learns from point-cloud inputs
without first training specialist policies, and achieves a 98.2%
overall success rate. Furthermore, the same framework can
train policies to complete 10 assembly pairs in sequence,
with a task reward issued only when all pairs are assembled
with no prescribed order. In other words, the policy exhibits
planning-like behavior.
Our contributions are the following:
1) A highly-effective multi-task RL framework con-
sisting of a novel combination of the following com-
ponents: A) generation of a cache of assembly-relevant
reset states [10] over heterogeneous assembly pairs, B)
success-guided sampling within the cache [7], C) a uni-
fied sparse binary task reward, D) multi-GPU training
at scale. These components are supported by careful
ablations. All tasks share one training configuration,
without per-task tuning or demonstrations.
2) The first multi-task RL policy capable of solving all
20 tasks on the NIST Assembly Task Board 1. The
policy takes point clouds as input; initial part poses
are sampled from a wide range, including adverse
conditions (e.g., upside-down); and each task can be
solved with near-perfect success rates. Positive transfer
is observed, where multi-task policies can solve the 20
tasks with higher success rate than individual special-
ists, including three tasks that the specialists struggle
to solve.
3) The first assembly policy capable of solving up
to 10 diverse assembly pairs in sequence, without
an explicit hierarchy or prescribed task order. Thus,
this work demonstrates that sequencing behavior can
emerge within a single low-level assembly policy.
We observe a repertoire of unique emergent behaviors
including dexterous skills such as pushing and sliding (to sin-
gulate parts), in-hand pivoting, multiple sequential regrasps,
extrinsic contact (to reorient parts prior to insertion), and
single-gripper nut threading. The single policy also exhibits
long-horizon planning-like behavior such as bringing parts
into reach, moving obstructing parts aside, and leaving a
task partially-finished and then returning to it later. None
of these skills and behaviors were specified. We encourage
readers to watch our supplementary videos 1 for additional
examples of these emergent behaviors. We plan to release
our code following the review and revision period.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Scalable Learning for Contact-Rich Assembly
Parallel physics simulation supports experience collection
at scale for on-policy methods such as PPO [11], [12], with
applications ranging from dexterous in-hand manipulation
[13], [14], [15], [16] to industrial assembly. GPU-accelerated
physics engines such as PhysX [17] and Newton [18] support
large-scale parallel simulation for robot learning. For indus-
trial assembly, Factory [2] demonstrated real-time or faster
simulation of 1,000 simultaneous nut-and-bolt interactions
in Isaac Gym, later incorporated into Isaac Lab. AlphaNIST
uses this simulation throughput to collect experience across
heterogeneous assembly pairs.
OmniReset [10] addressed state coverage through diverse,
manipulation-centric resets, demonstrating long-horizon on-
policy RL with fixed algorithm hyperparameters and no
curricula or human demonstrations. Success-Guided Sam-
pling (SGS) [7] addressed experience allocation by con-
centrating simulation near the policy’s changing capability
frontier, scaling beyond one million parallel environments
using sparse rewards. In its reported assembly experiments,
SGS trains a separate policy for each single task. AlphaNIST
retains SGS’s sampling rule but trains one multi-task policy
across all 20 ATB1 tasks under a common binary task reward.
Our experiments show that this shared policy learns assem-
blies that remain unsolved in single-task training (Fig. 5).

B. Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning
Multi-task RL aims to improve learning by sharing experi-
ence across tasks. However, standard joint-training baselines
have achieved lower success rates than separately trained
policies on multi-task manipulation benchmarks [19]. One
approach to obtaining a single multi-task policy is to distill
multiple separately trained specialists into a shared pol-
icy [20]. Other approaches address the difficulty of joint
training directly. PopArt [21] addresses differences in re-
ward scales with a separate value output and normaliza-
tion statistics for each task, while PCGrad [19] removes
conflicting components of task gradients before combining
them. However, Kurin et al. [22] show that shared-network
baselines with standard regularization and stabilization can
match specialized multi-task optimizers.
AlphaNIST takes a simple approach to multi-task learning
by exploiting the shared structure of assembly: threading,
gear meshing, and insertion all receive the same binary task
reward when assembly error falls below a common thresh-
old. Under this shared objective, we train one geometry-
conditioned policy and one value function with standard
PPO at scale, without task-specific value heads, gradient
projection, or specialist pretraining.
C. Learning-Based Robot Assembly
Prior learning-based assembly systems make exploration
tractable by shortening the task horizon or restricting initial-
ization to staged, pre-grasped, or local-insertion problems
[23], [24]. Factory [2] trains separate pick, place, and screw
subpolicies, while AutoMate [20] combines assembly-by-
disassembly, imitation learning, and curriculum-based RL
to obtain specialist and generalist assembly policies. Refin-
ery [25] improves pretrained assembly specialists through
Bayesian-optimization-guided sampling of initial conditions
during fine-tuning and Gaussian-mixture-model-based ini-
tialization at deployment. The refined policies can then
be chained to complete multi-part assemblies. For longer
sequences, ARCH [26] uses a high-level imitation-learned
policy to select from a library of RL and model-based
primitives. These methods structure learning through ini-
tialization, demonstrations, or prescribed subskills. OmniRe-
set [10] uses a generic dense objective with fixed weights,
including gripper–object and object–goal distance terms.
Zhang et al. [7] present the first end-to-end RL solution to
individual tasks from the NIST ATB1, but retain task-specific
auxiliary grasp- and gripper-related shaping. Such terms can
aid exploration and discourage reward exploitation, but their
relative influence can change with object size, geometry, and
assembly motion. Even with fixed coefficients, a new task
family requires checking that the shaped reward remains
aligned with successful assembly.
AlphaGo Zero provides a broader motivation: terminal
win/loss and search-generated policy targets support strategy
discovery without human game data or intermediate reward
shaping [9]. AlphaNIST asks whether binary task success can
similarly support strategy discovery in continuous, contact-
rich control, with one shared policy learning heterogeneous
assemblies end to end.

tasks with standard PPO and a binary task reward for
completed assembly. Its training distribution combines di-
verse cached reset states from OmniReset [10] with beta-
distributed success-guided sampling (SGS) [7], while a com-
mon binary task reward defines successful assembly across
all tasks. The three components play distinct roles:

E. Distributed Training and Scale
We scale synchronous PPO across 1–256K environments,
with 4K per GPU in multi-GPU runs. Each GPU samples its
local reset bank using rolling success estimates. Actor and
critic gradients are averaged across GPUs before each opti-
mizer step. Each iteration collects 32 steps per environment
and optimizes for 5 epochs with 4 minibatches each. Network
architecture and PPO settings are fixed across scales.

B. Sparse Binary Task Rewards Benefit from Scale
Performance with the sparse binary task reward improves
rapidly with increased parallel experience (Fig. 7). With
beta sampling, evaluation success rises from 38.6% at 1K
environments to 78.9% at 4K and 86.9% at 8K. With dense
reward shaping, success remains at 11.8–12.0% over 1K–
4K environments. Beta sampling provides an additional gain
over uniform reset sampling. At 256K environments, success
with the sparse binary task reward reaches 97.4% with
beta sampling versus 84.3% with uniform sampling; the
corresponding results with dense reward shaping are 77.4%
and 34.3%. While the advantage of the sparse task objective
is already visible at modest scale, beta sampling provides
further gains as parallel experience increases.
C. Emergent Strategies over Short Horizons
The policy exhibits a broad range of dexterous strategies
across assemblies (Fig. 8), including grasp adaptation, ex-
trinsic reorientation, and contact-driven assembly. It releases
and reacquires parts when the initial grasp is unsuitable
for insertion, uses surrounding geometry to change object
orientation, and discovers intermittent tapping and pivoting
motions. These behaviors emerge within a single policy,
without demonstrations or rewards specifying the individual
maneuvers. For a peg, a successful pickup may not produce
a grasp suitable for insertion; the policy drops a center-held
peg and regrasps it near one end, exposing enough shaft
length for complete insertion. For a nut, repeated taps on
one side engage and advance the thread, using intermittent
contact to make assembly progress.
D. Emergent Strategies over Long Horizons
Beyond the main single-assembly experiments, we train
policies with multiple assembly pairs per episode. The bi-

nary task reward is issued only when every active pair is
assembled; episodes allow 15 seconds per pair and terminate
upon success. Table I shows the success rates for episodes
with up to ten active pairs. This multi-pair setting reveals
coordination over substantially longer horizons (Fig. 9). The
policy need not finish assembling a pair before turning to
another, and its approach to one assembly changes based
on neighboring parts. These planning-like behaviors—taking
actions whose utility is realized later and adapting one
assembly to the state of another—emerge without an explicit
planner, policy switching, or prescribed sequence.
VI. LIMITATIONS
First, all results are in simulation. Hardware-matched
velocity and torque limits constrain the learned motions, but
do not establish robustness to perception error, latency, or
contact-model mismatch. Transfer and repeatability will be
evaluated on the physical board using sensor-derived obser-
vations and randomized contact and latency models. Second,
while applying the 10-pair policy to successive groups of
parts could offer a route to completing the full board,
extending our multi-pair framework to 20 parts remains an
exciting research goal. Achieving this at scale, under a single
terminal task reward, will likely benefit from more efficient
simulation and learning methods.
VII. CONCLUSION
AlphaNIST shows that heterogeneous contact-rich assem-
bly can be learned with a single policy using a binary
task objective, diverse reset coverage, and large-scale re-
inforcement learning. In simulation, one policy learns all
20 ATB1 tasks without demonstrations, prescribed stages,
or per-task tuning, while outperforming separately trained
specialists on the most difficult small-part assemblies. Across
all tested scales, the binary task objective outperforms the
evaluated dense reward shaping. The learned policy discovers
grasp adaptation, extrinsic reorientation, tapping, threading,
and longer-horizon coordination without direct supervision.
In multi-pair assembly, it can prepare one part, complete
another, and return to finish the first, without an intermediate
assembly reward or a planner prescribing the sequence.
More broadly, these findings challenge the assumption that
task complexity and diversity must be reduced to make robot
learning tractable. Instead, they show that diverse experience
under a shared objective can itself become a source of
reusable dexterity and coordination. We view this work as
a critical step toward general-purpose assembly policies that
discover how to solve complex physical tasks, rather than
requiring those strategies to be specified in advance.
