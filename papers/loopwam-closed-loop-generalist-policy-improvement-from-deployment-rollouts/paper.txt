Abstract
Scaling expert demonstrations alone does not enable generalist robot policies to improve from de-
ployment. Real-world rollouts contain successes, suboptimal behavior, and failures: imitating every
recorded action can reinforce poor behavior, while filtering out failures discards evidence of physical
interactions and their consequences. We introduce LoopWAM, a closed-loop world-action model that
learns directly from this full quality spectrum, using rollouts from both itself and other deployed policies.
Source-dependent noise sampling unifies behavior learning and action-conditioned world modeling,
while quality steering and quality-weighted supervision retain full video supervision across qualities
and regulate action imitation. Video and action tokens interact throughout parallel denoising, allow-
ing learned consequences to inform action generation directly. On RoboChallenge Table30-V2, three
deployment-and-training rounds raise success from 23.67% to 43.33%, a 19.7-percentage-point gain,
with a final score of 56.80. LoopWAM also achieves 94.5% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 and 49.5%
overall success on RoboCasa365 without additional embodied pretraining, obtaining the best overall
results among the compared methods across all three benchmarks. On challenging shirt folding task,
two rounds of deployment-driven improvement after single-task post-training raise success from 13.20%
to 55.56%. These results demonstrate closed-loop improvement from mixed-quality experience under
both multi-task learning and single-task post-training. Project Page
1. Introduction
Scaling human-collected robot data has expanded the capabilities of generalist manipulation policies (O’Neill
et al., 2024), but demonstration scaling alone does not close the loop between deployment and continual
improvement. Deployment exposes states, interactions, and failure modes beyond curated demonstrations,
providing new experience for an embodied data flywheel. Real-world reinforcement learning (RL) (Xu et al.,
2026) can improve policy performance during deployment, but may narrow the model’s predictive distribution
and often struggles to learn multi-task generalist policies. Although distilling a generalist policy from multiple RL
specialists (Xiao et al., 2025) can address this issue, the generalist’s supervision comes from curated successful
rollouts rather than the full interaction history. Therefore, we aim to study direct generalist improvement
from the full mixture of successful, suboptimal, and failed deployment rollouts. The challenge is to use this
experience productively: imitating every recorded action risks absorbing undesirable behavior, while retaining
only high-quality trajectories discards informative interactions and their outcomes.
Recent VLA and world-action models address important pieces of this problem. On the VLA side, 𝜋0.6 (Physical
Intelligence, 2025) uses advantage-conditioned learning to incorporate autonomous experience and interven-
tions, while 𝜋0.7 (Physical Intelligence, 2026) uses quality, speed, and mistake metadata to absorb mixed-quality
data and distill specialist capabilities into a generalist. These VLA methods use steering during training to model

LoopWAM: Closed-Loop Generalist Policy Improvement from Deployment Rollouts
We introduce LoopWAM, a closed-loop World-Action Model that updates a generalist directly from mixed-
quality deployment rollouts. Our core idea is to let one WAM directly absorb rollouts across the full quality
spectrum and learn from each. To achieve this, we first use source-dependent noise sampling to unify WAM
behavior learning and ACWM consequence learning within a single model and generation stage. Video and
action noise levels are sampled independently, allowing low-noise rollout actions to guide prediction of their
observed consequences. Within this unified model, quality steering represents behaviors of different qualities
and selects the highest-quality condition at deployment. To control how strongly each behavior is imitated, we
further apply quality-weighted supervision, reducing imitation of suboptimal actions and masking failed-action
targets while retaining video supervision across all qualities. Together, these mechanisms let every trajectory
supervise the joint model according to its behavioral quality and observed transitions. Video and action tokens
interact bidirectionally throughout one parallel denoising process, allowing learned future representations
to participate in action formation without a separate consequence-evaluation stage. We close the loop by
repeatedly collecting rollouts and retraining the generalist on demonstrations and accumulated experience
before redeployment. On RoboChallenge Table30-V2 (RoboChallenge Dexmal Team, 2026), three rounds
raise overall success from 23.67% to 28.00%, 37.67%, and 43.33%, a 19.7-percentage-point improvement,
with a final score of 56.80, ranking first in both success and score among the compared entries (Table 1).
LoopWAM achieves 94.5% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025), exceeding the reported
87.5% of FACT and 93.6% of LingBot-VA 2.0, and 49.5% on the 50-task RoboCasa365 evaluation (Nasiriany
et al., 2026), above ABot-M0.6’s 46.6% (Tables 2–3). We further evaluate deployment-driven self-improvement
after single-task post-training on challenging shirt folding, which involves deformable objects and multiple
coordinated steps. Two deployment-and-training rounds raise success from 13.20% to 55.56% (Section 4.4).
These results establish that directly learning from mixed-quality rollouts with parallel world–action denoising
can deliver leading benchmark performance and repeated deployment improvement in both multi-task and
single-task settings.
2. Related Work
Robot Manipulation Policies. Robot manipulation policies increasingly combine scalable imitation learning
with pretrained representations. ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) and Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2025) learn action
sequences from demonstrations, while RT-2 (Zitkovich et al., 2023) and OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) transfer
vision-language priors to language-conditioned control. Flow-based policies such as 𝜋0 (Black et al., 2024)
and 𝜋0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) further scale continuous action generation and generalization.
Open X-Embodiment (O’Neill et al., 2024) and DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) expand training coverage
across embodiments and environments. Beyond offline demonstrations, DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) addresses
policy-induced distribution shift by collecting expert labels on visited states. LoopWAM studies learning from
the recorded observations and actions of deployment rollouts, using trajectory quality to guide policy updates
while retaining world supervision from unsuccessful interactions.
World Action Models. Video-based robot policies use predictive visual representations to support control.
mimic-video (Pai et al., 2025) conditions an action decoder on representations from a pretrained video model.
DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026b) jointly generates video and actions, while LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026) organizes
visual prediction and action decoding within a causal autoregressive framework. LingBot-VA 2.0 (Zhang et al.,
2026a) further develops native video-action pretraining with a control-oriented tokenizer. Efficiency-oriented
designs include Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026), which retains video co-training but removes future-video
generation at inference, and GigaWorld-Policy (Ye et al., 2026a), which learns action-conditioned visual
dynamics while allowing action-only inference. Being-H0.7 (Luo et al., 2026) instead uses future observations
to supervise latent representations. These studies establish several ways for predictive supervision to support
action learning. LoopWAM focuses on closing the deployment–training loop: mixed-quality rollouts broaden
world-model training, while joint video–action modeling, quality steering, and weighted action supervision
connect consequence awareness with high-quality behavior.

3. Method
LoopWAM learns a world–action model that can be updated with experience collected during deployment.
The framework couples video and action generation within a shared denoising process, then uses trajectory
quality to distinguish how deployment data supervise world modeling and control. Figure 2 summarizes the
architecture and training procedure. We first introduce joint world–action modeling, followed by quality-aware
learning from mixed trajectories and the deployment–learning loop.

3.2. Quality-Aware Learning from Mixed Trajectories
Our training corpus combines expert demonstrations 𝒟𝐸with rollout data 𝒟𝑅of arbitrary quality, including
successful, suboptimal, and failed executions. These rollouts substantially broaden the state–action–outcome
distribution beyond expert paths, providing world-model supervision even when their actions are undesirable
imitation targets. Each trajectory is assigned a source indicator 𝑑∈{𝐸, 𝑅}; every training segment extracted
from that trajectory uses the same indicator. We combine source-dependent noise sampling, quality conditioning,
and selective action supervision to learn from this broader experience while favoring high-quality behavior.
Source-dependent noise sampling. Jointly predicting video and actions in a WAM does not by itself ensure
that the predicted futures reflect the consequences of the generated actions. To improve consistency between
video and action generation and make world predictions more useful for control, we aim to strengthen action-
conditioned world modeling (ACWM) within the same unified model. We therefore use source-dependent
noise sampling to make recorded rollout actions accessible when predicting their observed consequences,
while retaining joint video–action generation in a single training stage. Specifically, we select different noise
distributions for expert and rollout segments, while drawing video and action noise levels independently within
each segment:

use an action DiT with a hidden dimension of 1024. Each training segment pairs a 64-step action chunk with

4.2. Benchmark Experimental Setup
We evaluate LoopWAM on the real-robot benchmark RoboChallenge Table30-V2 and two simulation benchmarks,
RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboCasa365. RoboChallenge evaluates closed-loop self-improvement using LoopWAM’s
own real-robot deployments. The two simulation benchmarks additionally test learning from different deployed
policies by augmenting expert demonstrations with rollouts from both expert-trained LoopWAM and others.
Across these settings, successful, suboptimal, and failed rollouts expand the state–action–outcome distribution
beyond expert demonstrations.
RoboChallenge Table30-V2.
RoboChallenge Table30-V2 (RoboChallenge Dexmal Team, 2026) is a standardized real-robot benchmark
comprising 30 tabletop manipulation tasks across four embodiments: ALOHA, DOS-W1, ARX5, and UR5. Its
tasks test capabilities including tool use, precise manipulation, and coordinated bimanual control. We follow
the official multi-task evaluation protocol and report overall performance across embodiments. Starting from
one expert-trained multi-task LoopWAM per embodiment, we conduct three successive rounds of closed-loop
self-improvement. In each deployment round, we run each model on its assigned tasks and collect about 100
rollout trajectories per task. These trajectories are quality-annotated and combined with expert demonstrations
and rollout trajectories from all previous deployment rounds for further training.
RoboTwin 2.0.
RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025) comprises 50 bimanual manipulation tasks evaluated in clean and domain-
randomized scenes. Following the multi-task setup of LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026), we use 2,500 expert
demonstrations from clean scenes and 25,000 from heavily randomized scenes. We augment these demon-
strations with rollouts generated by expert-trained LoopWAM, X-VLA (Zheng et al., 2025), and 𝜋0.5 (Physical
Intelligence et al., 2025). For each task, each policy contributes 20 clean and 130 randomized rollouts, yielding
22,500 trajectories across all 50 tasks and the three policies. The combined rollout corpus has approximately
the same total duration as the expert corpus. Rollout collection and evaluation use different random seeds to
avoid data leakage. Rollout quality is annotated using the coarse-level numerical scores in Section 3.2.
RoboCasa365.
RoboCasa365 (Nasiriany et al., 2026) is a large-scale simulation framework for household manipulation in
diverse kitchen environments. We evaluate on its 50-task benchmark, which includes atomic and composite
manipulation tasks. Training combines expert demonstrations with rollouts generated by expert-trained
LoopWAM and RLDX-1 (Kim et al., 2026), with the combined rollout duration approximately matching that of
the expert data. Both expert and rollout data follow the Human300 training split: the 16 Composite-Unseen
tasks are excluded from training and evaluated zero-shot. As in RoboTwin 2.0, training and evaluation data
use different random seeds.

5. Conclusion
We presented LoopWAM for improving generalist world-action policies directly from deployment experience. The
key is to learn physical consequences from trajectories across the quality spectrum while controlling how their
actions supervise behavior. Source-dependent noise sampling, quality steering, and quality-weighted supervision
implement this distinction within joint video–action denoising, allowing consequence representations to
participate directly in action generation. The same formulation accepts rollouts from both LoopWAM and
other deployed policies. Experiments show the best overall results: a score of 56.80 and 43.33% success on
RoboChallenge Table30-V2, 94.5% average success on RoboTwin 2.0, and 49.5% overall success on RoboCasa365.
Repeated real-robot deployment raises Table30-V2 success by 19.7 percentage points over three rounds and
improves shirt-folding success from 13.20% to 55.56% over two rounds after single-task post-training. These
findings support using mixed-quality interaction history as a training resource for continued improvement of
both multi-task policies and policies adapted to individual tasks.

A. Training and Data Processing Details
A.1. Trajectory-Quality Annotations
We use two numerical quality scales according to annotation granularity. Episode-level annotation uses a 1–12
scale, with rollout scores in [1, 10] and expert demonstrations assigned 𝑞⋆= 12. Coarse-level annotation uses a
1–5 scale, with rollout scores in {1, 2, 3, 4} and expert demonstrations assigned 𝑞⋆= 5. In all settings, every
failed trajectory receives 𝑞= 1. Expert demonstrations are treated as optimal reference trajectories and assigned
scores above the rollout range; the same expert score is used at deployment. Each score is assigned to a
complete trajectory and inherited by all training segments extracted from it.
RoboChallenge Table30-V2. We use human episode-level annotations from the official RoboChallenge platform.
Platform operators already rate each rollout on a 1–10 scale according to task completion, with successful
trajectories typically receiving scores of at least 8.5. We directly use these platform ratings as rollout quality
scores, except that all failed trajectories are assigned 𝑞= 1. Expert demonstrations receive 𝑞⋆= 12.
Single-task shirt-folding post-training. We likewise use human episode-level annotation. After each rollout,
the data collector assesses task completion before resetting the scene and assigns a score on a 1–10 scale,
following the same completion-based scoring approach as RoboChallenge. Failed trajectories receive 𝑞= 1, and
task-specific expert demonstrations receive 𝑞⋆= 12.
RoboTwin 2.0. We apply coarse-level annotation separately to rollouts from expert-trained LoopWAM, X-VLA,
and 𝜋0.5. For each policy, we compute its overall rollout success rate across all tasks and manually inspect
approximately 0.7% of its trajectories for motion smoothness and action accuracy. These two assessments
determine a single shared score for all successful trajectories from that policy across tasks; every failed trajectory
receives 𝑞= 1. Expert-trained LoopWAM achieves approximately 90% success during rollout collection and
produces relatively smooth trajectories, so its successful rollouts receive 𝑞= 4. X-VLA and 𝜋0.5 each achieve
less than 50% success and produce less smooth trajectories, so their successful rollouts receive 𝑞= 3. Expert
demonstrations receive 𝑞⋆= 5.
RoboCasa365. We use the same coarse-level protocol, assessing each source policy through its overall rollout
success rate across tasks and manual inspection of approximately 0.7% of its trajectories for motion smoothness
and action accuracy. We obtain two expert-trained LoopWAM policies using 64-step and 32-step action chunks,
denoted Policy 1 and Policy 2, respectively, and also collect rollouts from RLDX-1. Following the same scoring
criteria as RoboTwin, all successful rollouts from Policy 1 share 𝑞= 3, those from Policy 2 share 𝑞= 4, and
those from RLDX-1 share 𝑞= 2. All failed rollouts receive 𝑞= 1, and expert demonstrations receive 𝑞⋆= 5.
A.2. Training Details
Temporal alignment and robot representations. The training configurations use an action horizon of 𝐻= 64
and a visual stride of eight control steps, yielding nine RGB frames including the initial observation. Camera
views are stitched into a single image, and robot states and actions are normalized using expert-data statistics.
For Table30-V2, joint targets are represented relative to the joint state at the beginning of each action chunk,
whereas gripper commands remain absolute. Only the joint components are converted back to absolute targets
at execution; the gripper components are not differenced. To reduce redundant stationary data, the real-world
pipeline checks unnormalized state sequences with an absolute tolerance of 10−3 and resamples nearly static
windows up to three times. Windows starting within the final 20 seconds of expert episodes are exempt from
this filtering to retain terminal behavior. Padding is excluded from the denoising losses.
Noise schedules and loss weighting. The base noise sampler uses 𝜑5(𝑢) = 5𝑢/(1 + 4𝑢) for 𝑢∼𝒰(0, 1).
